How Task Partitioning and Communication Limit Multi-Agent Collaboration

2026-01-31 · agentsmulti-agentinformation-theorypaper

Paper: How Task Structure Limits Multi-Agent Success: An Information-Theoretic Analysis, with Dr. Ming Luo. This post explains the main results through examples. The figures, tables and formulas come from the paper. The section on multi-turn agents is my further interpretation, beyond the theorems themselves.

Why three reasonable patches can fail together

Suppose fixing a bug requires changes to a parser, a validator and the test that covers both. The parser must produce what the validator expects, and the test must check that they agree. Give the three files to engineers who cannot see each other's diffs, and their individually reasonable patches may fail when merged. The difficulty lies in the dependencies between the files: the edges of the graph.

Multi-agent systems (MAS) divide work to ease the limitations of a single agent. Yet on some complex benchmarks, they perform worse than one agent using the same model. Coordination costs, error propagation and redundant roles explain part of this. Our paper asks whether the structure of the task also places a limit on successful collaboration.

The part of a task that resists division

We represent the task as a constraint graph. Nodes are decisions, and edges are constraints between them. Each edge has an entropy $H(\phi_e)$ describing its information content. Some parts of a task can be completed independently; others contain dense dependencies. The paper calls the latter region the irreducible bottleneck $\tau_{\text{hard}}$, or the core here.

Dividing the core among $m$ agents cuts constraints between their partitions. The smallest information cut across the possible partitions is

$$C_{\min} := \min_{\mathcal{P}} \sum_{e \in E_{\mathrm{cut}}(\mathcal{P})} H(\phi_e)$$

$C_{\min}$ measures how many bits of constraint information must be cut even with the best division of work. In this formulation, it is determined by task structure.

The paper compares closed-form expressions with brute-force minimum cuts on six common graph families. A chain needs only one edge cut; the eight-node complete graph requires sixteen under the partition considered. This illustrates why sequential work is easier to divide than work with dense dependencies.

How missing information limits success

Cut edges correspond to constraints that no agent holds in full. Under the paper's assumptions, the information missing about the correct solution $\tau^\star$ is at least proportional to the cut:

$$H(\tau^\star \mid \mathrm{Internal}) \ \ge\ c_1 \cdot C_{\min}$$

Internal denotes all the information the agents receive at the start. The gap therefore exists before execution begins.

Without the constraint information, the system has to guess part of the answer. Intuitively, guessing $k$ unknown bits correctly has a probability of about $2^{-k}$. Theorem 1 gives the corresponding upper bound under explicit assumptions:

$$P(\text{success}) = O\!\left(2^{-\Omega(C_{\min} - H_{\mathrm{sol}})}\right)$$

$H_{\mathrm{sol}}$ accounts for tasks with multiple acceptable answers. It is zero for a unique answer, leaving a bound that falls exponentially with the cut.

In a controlled three-colour graph-colouring experiment, the predicted loss is $\log_2(3/2) \approx 0.585$ bits per cut edge. The measured gap agrees:

The bound relies on typicality assumptions. Cut constraints must not duplicate information already available to the agents, and the solution space must not offer shortcuts that make guessing much easier than the entropy predicts. These assumptions define what counts as a core here. Tasks that violate them may admit restructuring and have a smaller effective bottleneck.

Can internal communication close the gap?

A natural response is to let the agents communicate more. The paper considers a closed system, which receives no external observations during the run. For any number of communication rounds $T$:

$$I(\tau^\star;\ \mathrm{Internal}, \mathcal{M}^{(1:T)}) = I(\tau^\star;\ \mathrm{Internal})$$

Here, each message is produced from its sender's information and earlier messages. Communication can redistribute what the system knows, but it cannot supply information about the solution that the system never had. More bandwidth therefore leaves the bound unchanged. The synthetic experiment in the left panel uses a grid with $C_{\min}=4$: success stays near one third as communication rises from zero to a hundred bits.

The lemma applies to closed systems whose messages depend only on local information and previous messages. It concerns an upper bound on success. Communication can still help agents make better use of available information and improve performance below that bound.

What external feedback changes

Interaction with the environment supplies new information. Let $F$ denote feedback bits obtained through actions, such as a failed test, a compiler error or a user correction, and $R$ the bits of that feedback returned to the planner. Theorem 3 gives:

$$P(\text{success}) \le O\!\left(2^{-\max(0,\ c\,C_{\min} - F - R - H_{\mathrm{sol}})}\right)$$

The exponent represents the remaining information gap. When $F + R$ reaches $c\,C_{\min} - H_{\mathrm{sol}}$, this bound no longer requires success to be exponentially unlikely. The right panel shows the change on the same grid: success rises from about one third without feedback to over ninety percent at 1.6 bits, with every trial succeeding at 3.2 bits. The observed transition is close to the predicted 1.29 bits.

The distinction is whether the information was previously missing from the system. Internal communication redistributes existing knowledge; external feedback can reduce the gap caused by partitioning.

Where multi-turn agents fit (my reading)

The paper compares multi-agent and single-agent systems. I also want to use it to think about when to divide a task and when to let one agent keep iterating.

The paper assumes a single-agent capacity $W$: the task-relevant information an agent can actually use in one execution. When the core exceeds that capacity, task partitioning and repeated execution are two possible responses.

Partitioning introduces the information gap measured by $C_{\min}$. Under the closed-system assumptions, internal communication alone cannot recover it.

Alternatively, one agent can execute, receive feedback and execute again. The useful information gained per round is limited by the feedback channel. On SWE-bench, the paper uses test-outcome entropy as a proxy for $F$; a pass/fail signal carries at most one bit. This suggests a rough turn budget: divide $(c\,C_{\min} - H_{\mathrm{sol}})$ by the useful information returned per round. A stack trace is usually more informative than a failure flag and may reduce the rounds needed. This makes me pay attention to what tools return, alongside how much context the agent can hold.

This reading has limits. The paper does not model turns explicitly, and useful information per round is difficult to measure in a real agent. It offers one way to interpret something I have observed: tasks that defeat multi-agent collaboration can also require many iterations from a single agent. Both may be affected by the same task bottleneck.

Testing the predictions on real models

The synthetic agents satisfy the assumptions by construction; real LLM agents may not. The paper therefore tests four directional predictions on SWE-bench Lite, using 46 systems (18 multi-agent, 18 single-agent and 10 non-agentic), 300 tasks and about fifteen thousand result records. For each task, $C_{\min}$ is estimated from a dependency graph over the lines touched by the ground-truth patch, using an exact two-way minimum cut.

All four results agree with the predicted direction and are statistically significant, but the effect sizes are modest. The paper discusses several possible reasons. Real systems may operate far below the upper bound. LLM agents share training distributions and may coordinate implicitly, weakening the non-redundancy assumption. Familiar code patterns may also offer shortcuts; the no-shortcuts condition has not been verified for these tasks. The results support further study of task structure, but do not establish a calibrated predictor of success.

Implications for system design

  • Examine dependencies before assigning work. Tasks with low $C_{\min}$ are better candidates for parallel execution; densely connected tasks need more care.
  • For a large core, try restructuring the task to reduce constraints across agents.
  • Design tools for informative feedback and allow enough budget for useful iterations.
  • Compare multi-agent division and single-agent iteration in terms of task structure, capacity and feedback quality.
