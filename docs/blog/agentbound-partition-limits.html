Limits from Task Partitioning and Communication Bottlenecks

2026-09-08 · agentsmulti-agentinformation-theorypaper

Paper: How Task Structure Limits Multi-Agent Success: An Information-Theoretic Analysis, with Ming Luo. This post is the plain-language version. The figure, the tables and the formulas are lifted from the paper; the reading of multi-turn agents in the second-to-last section is mine and goes a little beyond what the theorems literally say.

A bug that three people cannot fix

Imagine a bug whose fix touches three places: a parser, a validator, and the test that exercises both. Whatever the parser emits, the validator has to accept, and the test pins both down. Now hand the three files to three engineers who cannot see each other's diff. Each will write a perfectly reasonable patch. The merged result fails, because the thing that made the task hard was never inside any one file. It lived on the edges between them.

Multi-agent systems (MAS) are sold as the escape from single-agent limits: split the task, give each piece to an agent, combine. The empirical record is awkward. On complex benchmarks, MAS routinely lose to a single agent running the same model. The usual explanations are engineering ones: coordination overhead, error propagation, redundant roles. Our claim is that underneath the engineering there is a property of the task itself, and it sets a ceiling no architecture can lift.

Every task has a core

Write the task as a constraint graph. Nodes are the decisions you have to make; an edge joins two decisions that constrain each other; each edge carries an entropy $H(\phi_e)$, the amount of information in that constraint. Most real tasks have a separable outer layer and a hard inner region where every decision depends on several others. The paper calls that region the irreducible bottleneck $\tau_{\text{hard}}$. I will just call it the core.

Split the core among $m$ agents. Any partition cuts some edges. The cost of the cheapest possible split is

$$C_{\min} := \min_{\mathcal{P}} \sum_{e \in E_{\mathrm{cut}}(\mathcal{P})} H(\phi_e)$$

That is the whole idea in one line. $C_{\min}$ is how many bits of constraint you are forced to sever no matter how cleverly you divide the work. It is a number that belongs to the task, not to the framework.

How big does it get? The paper checks the closed-form value on six textbook graph families against a brute-force minimum cut. A chain splits at one edge. A fully connected graph on eight nodes cannot be split without severing sixteen. That is the gap between pipeline-shaped tasks, which divide cleanly, and everything-constrains-everything tasks, which do not.

Splitting the core costs you exponentially

The cut edges are constraints that nobody holds in full. The first result says the information the system is missing about the correct solution $\tau^\star$ is at least proportional to the cut:

$$H(\tau^\star \mid \mathrm{Internal}) \ \ge\ c_1 \cdot C_{\min}$$

where Internal is everything the agents were handed at the start. This gap is fixed by the task before any agent runs.

Missing bits become failure probability. To succeed you must guess the bits you do not have, and guessing $k$ bits correctly happens with probability about $2^{-k}$. Theorem 1 makes that precise:

$$P(\text{success}) = O\!\left(2^{-\Omega(C_{\min} - H_{\mathrm{sol}})}\right)$$

$H_{\mathrm{sol}}$ is a discount for tasks with many acceptable answers. If the answer is unique it is zero, and success decays exponentially in the cut alone.

Under controlled conditions the relation is exact. In a graph-colouring testbed with three colours, each cut edge should remove $\log_2(3/2) \approx 0.585$ bits, and that is what the measured information gap shows:

The bound needs a few "typicality" conditions: the cut constraints must not be secretly redundant with what the agents already have, and the solution space must not contain shortcuts that let you guess far better than the entropy predicts. Those conditions are the formal version of "this really is the core". If a task violates them, it can be restructured and its true core is smaller than it looks.

Talking more does not help, in a closed system

The obvious fix is to let the agents talk. Here is the result I find most counter-intuitive. In a closed system, one with no contact with the outside world during the run, for any number of rounds $T$:

$$I(\tau^\star;\ \mathrm{Internal}, \mathcal{M}^{(1:T)}) = I(\tau^\star;\ \mathrm{Internal})$$

Every message is a function of what its sender already knew. Communication can redistribute information across agents; it cannot create information about the solution that none of them had. So the bound from the previous section does not move when you add bandwidth. The left panel below is the synthetic check: a grid with $C_{\min}=4$, success rate flat near one third whether the agents exchange zero bits or a hundred.

Two caveats the paper is careful about. The lemma is for closed systems and for protocols where a message depends only on local knowledge and earlier messages. And it is a statement about the ceiling, not about everything below it: communication can absolutely help agents use what they have. It just cannot pay the cut.

Feedback is the only currency that pays

Now open the system. Let $F$ be the bits of feedback the agents obtain by executing something against the world (a failing test, a compiler error, a user's correction) and $R$ the bits of that feedback that make it back to whoever plans. Theorem 3:

$$P(\text{success}) \le O\!\left(2^{-\max(0,\ c\,C_{\min} - F - R - H_{\mathrm{sol}})}\right)$$

The exponent is the residual gap. Feedback bits subtract directly from the cut, and when $F + R$ reaches $c\,C_{\min} - H_{\mathrm{sol}}$ the exponent hits zero and success stops being exponentially unlikely. That is a phase transition, and the right panel above shows it on the same grid: from one third at zero feedback to over ninety percent at 1.6 bits, and to certainty by 3.2 bits, with the measured critical point near the predicted 1.29 bits.

So the asymmetry is real. Bits that agents pass to each other are worth nothing against the cut. Bits that come in from outside are worth exactly face value.

Where multi-turn agents fit (my reading)

The paper is framed as multi-agent versus single-agent. I think the cleaner statement is that the core presents one bill, and there are two currencies to pay it in.

A single agent has a capacity $W$, the bits of task-relevant information it can actually use in one execution; that is the paper's first assumption, and it is the real reason people split tasks at all. If the core does not fit in $W$, you have two moves.

The first is to split. You pay in cut, $C_{\min}$ bits, and in a closed loop that payment is never refunded.

The second is to keep one agent and iterate: run, get feedback, run again. Now you pay in feedback bits, $F$ per round. Each round buys at most as many bits as the feedback channel carries. On SWE-bench the paper uses the entropy of the test outcome as its proxy for $F$, and a pass/fail signal carries at most one bit. Read the phase-transition line as a turn budget: the number of informative rounds you need is at least $(c\,C_{\min} - H_{\mathrm{sol}})$ divided by the bits each round brings back. Below that budget you are on the red side of the figure, and no amount of prompt engineering moves you to the green side. Richer feedback, a stack trace instead of a red cross, raises the bits per round and shortens the budget. That is why long-horizon agents live or die on the quality of their tools rather than on the size of their context.

This is an interpretation, not a theorem. The paper does not model turns explicitly, and a real agent's feedback per round is not a clean number. But it explains something I keep seeing: the tasks where a multi-agent system fails are the same tasks where a single agent needs an unreasonable number of iterations. Same core, two currencies.

Does it survive contact with real models?

The synthetic agents obey the assumptions by construction. Real LLM agents do not, so the paper tests four directional predictions on SWE-bench Lite: 46 systems (18 multi-agent, 18 single-agent, 10 non-agentic), 300 tasks, about fifteen thousand result records. $C_{\min}$ for each task is estimated by building a dependency graph over the lines the ground-truth patch touches and taking an exact two-way minimum cut.

All four point the right way and all four are significant. The effect sizes are modest, and the paper says so. Three reasons, in decreasing order of comfort: the theorems are upper bounds, so real systems are allowed to sit well below them; LLM agents share a training distribution and may coordinate implicitly, which chips at the non-redundancy condition; and nobody has verified the no-shortcuts condition for code, where familiar patterns really can let a model guess a fix without the constraint information. Treat the SWE-bench numbers as evidence that the structure is there, not as a calibrated predictor.

What I take from it

  • Measure the core before choosing an architecture. If a task partitions at low $C_{\min}$, agents are cheap parallelism. If it does not, they are an exponential tax.
  • When the core is large, restructure the task. Adding agents or letting them talk more does not touch the exponent.
  • Budget feedback, not tokens. The quantity that moves an agent system across the phase transition is bits from the world, per round, and how many rounds you can afford.
  • Multi-agent and multi-turn are the same bill in two currencies. Pick the one whose exchange rate your task can afford.
